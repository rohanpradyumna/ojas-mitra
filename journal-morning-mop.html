On the smell of the morning mop.

There is a particular smell that fills an Indian house in the five minutes between when the floor has been mopped and when it has dried. I do not think we appreciate it enough. It is the smell of a clean room, but a clean room of a specific kind — warm, slightly herbal, faintly damp, with neem somewhere underneath. It is not the smell of a hotel lobby or a hospital corridor, which announce their cleanliness as a kind of warning.

My mother used to put a few drops of phenyl into a bucket of water. The whole house would smell of it for the rest of the morning. It was not unpleasant, exactly — we associated it with safety, with hands that took care of the home — but it was also not a smell that belonged to anywhere in the natural world. It was a smell that meant this floor has been treated.

When we were making the floor cleaner, this is what we had to think about. What does a house smell like when it has been cleaned well? Not when it has been treated, but when it has been cared for? The answer, for us, came down to three things.

Neem, lemon, eucalyptus

The neem is from the tree across the lane. It does the gentle antimicrobial work that the old village houses relied on for centuries, without needing to be aggressive about it.

The lemon is the bright top note. It is what you smell first, and what tells you, immediately, that the room is fresher than it was a minute ago.

The eucalyptus is the surprise. It is what carries through — what hangs in the air for half an hour after the floor is dry — and it is what people write to us about. I walked into the room half an hour after I’d mopped it, said one woman, and I stopped because I had forgotten I had done it. It felt as though the house had cleaned itself.

The five minutes

The floor cleaner is one capful in a bucket of water. Mopping is the same as it has always been, the same long quiet movement back and forth. What changes is what happens after.

I have started, in the mornings, to sit in the room I have just mopped for those five minutes between damp and dry. The light comes in differently when the floor is wet. The smell rises around you. It is one of the small, almost unnoticed rewards of keeping a home, and I think we have a right to make it slightly more beautiful.
