Kumkuma, in sixteen herbs.

I have wanted to make Kumkumadhi tailam since I was eleven. My grandmother had a small brass bottle of it on her dressing table, behind the comb and the kumkum and the steel box of pins. She would use three drops on her face at night, with a quiet expression I associated with the end of the day. When she passed, the bottle came to my mother, who gave it to me when I left home. I used the last of it in 2019.

Kumkumadhi tailam is one of the oldest preparations in classical Ayurveda. It appears in the Ashtanga Hridaya, written in the seventh century, and probably much earlier than that — the recipe in the text feels copied down from somewhere older. The name comes from kumkuma, which is saffron, and adi, which means and others. There are sixteen others, depending on which manuscript you read; saffron is only the first.

What is in the bottle we make

Saffron (Kashmiri Mongra, more expensive than we would like). Sandalwood, dissolved into the oil over weeks. Manjishtha root. Vetiver. Lotus stamen. Banyan bark. Indian madder. Liquorice. Indian rose petals. Cold-pressed sesame oil from a press in the next town. Goat’s milk, briefly, in one stage. Sixteen plants in all.

What is not in it: synthetic colour, synthetic fragrance, mineral oil, preservatives, parabens. The amber colour comes from the saffron, the sandalwood, and the slow heat. The smell comes from the herbs themselves; mostly sandalwood and rose, with the saffron quiet underneath.

How we make it

The process takes about seven weeks. The herbs are crushed and steeped first in goat’s milk — this seems strange, but the old books are insistent, and we have tried it without and the oil is not the same. The milk is then strained and the herbs go into the sesame oil, in a clay pot, over a low fire. The fire stays low for three hours each day, for four days. The oil is turned and watched. Then it rests for two weeks in the shade. Then it is filtered, twice, through cotton.

We make about thirty bottles every two months. That is all we can make at this pace; we are trying not to make any more, in the way that we are trying not to do many things.

How to use it

Three to five drops, at night, on a freshly washed face. Massage gently for a minute, with the palms or the fingertips. Leave on overnight. Wash in the morning with a mild cleanser. (Our hand wash, if you keep one near the basin, will do.)

It is not a serum. It does not work in a week. It is a tailam, which means it works the way oils work — slowly, cumulatively, the way old furniture comes to a polish over years. Use it for three months, then look at the difference. Keep using it after that, if you like.

The bottle I sent my daughter, this Diwali, was the first one I felt fully proud of. I think my grandmother would have approved.

— K
