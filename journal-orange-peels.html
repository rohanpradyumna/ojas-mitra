What we throw away when we throw away orange peels.

In our village, oranges arrive in March. They are not the supermarket kind. They are smaller, more sour, and sold by the bag from a cart that comes through the lane on Tuesdays. By April, every household has a small mountain of peels, and most of them go to the cows or into the compost or into the corner of the courtyard where things wait to be dealt with.

Two years ago, before I started bottling anything, my friend Padma asked me what would happen if I put them into a clay pot with jaggery and water instead. The way they used to, she said, vaguely, meaning her grandmother. She was right and she was wrong — people used to do many things; not always this one.

What happens is this. The sugars in the jaggery, the water, and the peels begin to interact. After a week the pot smells sharp and a little alarming. After three weeks it smells sweeter. After six weeks it smells almost like a kombucha, and after twelve weeks — ninety days, the number I have come to live by — it is a quiet, slightly acidic, slightly soapy liquid that, when diluted, cleans almost everything.

It does not announce itself. There are no fumes. There is no foam unless you add it on purpose. The liquid is brown-orange, the colour of weak tea, and when you spill a little on the kitchen counter, you can wipe it up with a cloth and forget it was there.

What it isn’t

I want to be plain about this. Bio-enzymes are not a miracle. They are not stronger than bleach. They will not get out the kind of stain that has been sitting in a shirt for six months. They are slow, and they ask for time — a minute on a counter, ten minutes on a tile, a soak overnight for a difficult pan.

But almost all of the cleaning a family does, day to day, is gentle work that someone has convinced us needs to be aggressive. We mop floors that are already mostly clean. We wash glasses that have held water. We do laundry that has been worn for an afternoon. For this work — which is most of it — you do not need anything stronger than a quiet, patient liquid.

What I make from it

Almost everything on my shelf is built on this base. The dishwash, the floor cleaner, the laundry liquid, the toilet cleaner, the vegetables and fruits cleanser, the hand wash. Six of seven bottles. Each one starts here — with peels I used to throw away — and then takes on a particular character: lemongrass for the dishwash, neem and eucalyptus for the floor, lavender and reetha for the laundry.

The Kumkumadhi tailam is the exception. It belongs to a different tradition entirely, and I will write about that another time.

The other thing the peels taught me is patience. There is no way to make a bio-enzyme faster than ninety days. I tried, the first year. The pots that I hurried tasted wrong — sharper, harsher, less alive. So now I wait. The pots in my courtyard are labelled with the date they began. The oldest is from February.

If you keep oranges in your kitchen, save the peels next week. There are instructions everywhere on the internet, but the recipe is simple: three parts peels, one part jaggery, ten parts water, ninety days, in a clay pot, in the shade. Stir once a week. Trust it.
